Chapter One: The Lamplighter's Daughter

by E. M. Harrow · The Wick Chronicle

The harbour town of Wick put itself to bed early. By the time the fishing boats had tied up along the quay, the only lights left burning were the lamps along Cobble Street, and those belonged to Mara Hale, who had lit them every evening since her father's hands began to shake too badly to hold the taper. She did not mind the work. The town was small enough that she knew every window she passed, and the lamps gave her an excuse to walk slowly past the ones she liked.

Tonight, however, the wind had come in from the north with a smell of iron in it, and the gulls had gone quiet an hour before dusk. Mara noticed both things and said nothing about either. She carried the long brass taper in one hand and a rag of mutton fat in the other, lifting the glass on each lamp in turn, coaxing the wick, and counting under her breath. Forty-one lamps on Cobble Street. Forty-one small yellow rooms hanging in the dark, each one a promise that somebody in Wick was still awake.

At the top of the street, where the cobbles gave way to the cliff path, she stopped. Out beyond the breakwater a ship was waiting with her lanterns dark, riding the swell without any sail set. No boat came into Wick after dark; the channel was too narrow and the rocks too patient. Yet there she sat, black against a blacker sea, and Mara had the distinct and uncomfortable impression that the ship was waiting for her.

She finished the last lamp, climbed the tower stairs, and set the flame in its housing. The glass was old and full of bubbles, and through it the harbour looked like something seen from the bottom of a bottle. Her father had told her once that the lamplighters of Wick were not paid to make light. They were paid to make sure the light did not go out. "Anyone can strike a match," he had said, bent over the kitchen table with his swollen knuckles wrapped around a cup. "A lamplighter is somebody the dark can count on."

On the shelf beneath the lamp she found a letter that had not been there at noon. It was folded twice and sealed with wax the colour of a deep bruise, and it carried no name on the outside, only a drawing of a ship with her lanterns dark. Mara read it twice, folded it once, and put it in her coat. Then she went down the stairs without hurrying, because a lamplighter does not run, and walked home along the harbour where the water was already beginning to shine like something newly forged.
