Chapter Two: The Salt Road

by E. M. Harrow · The Wick Chronicle

Morning found Mara on the cliff road with the letter in her pocket and a loaf of bread wrapped in a cloth under her arm. The wind had not died; it had merely changed sides, and now it pushed at her back as if it had somewhere to be. Below her, the town of Wick was a scatter of grey roofs and grey smoke, and beyond it the dark ship still waited, though the fishing boats had gone out at dawn and every man on the quay had sworn he had never seen her come in.

The letter was short. It named a buyer in Saltsend, three days' walk along the coast, and it said that the lamp at the north pier had been burning for a hundred and twelve years without once going out. It did not say who had written it, or how the writer had got into the tower, or why any of this should concern the daughter of a lamplighter. On the back, in a smaller hand, someone had added: Ask for the keeper of the sixth lamp.

The road to Saltsend ran through the salt pans, where the sea was let into shallow squares of clay and left to dry into something that could be sold. Mara had never been this far up the coast. She passed carts loaded with grey crystal, men with faces burned the colour of old leather, and a dog that followed her for a mile and then thought better of it. Nobody asked where she was going. On the salt road, everyone was going somewhere and nobody had time to be curious.

By evening she reached an inn with a sign that showed a ship and no lanterns. The innkeeper poured her a bowl of fish stew without being asked and told her that the room at the top of the stairs was free, though it had been occupied the night before by a man who had left without paying. "Tall fellow," the innkeeper said. "Hair like wet straw. Asked about the lamps." Mara ate her stew slowly and did not say that she had walked three days to ask about them too.

She was halfway up the stairs when the door opened behind her and the wind came in with a stranger in a grey coat. He was not tall, and his hair was not straw, and he carried no bag. But he looked at the letter in her hand as though he had written it himself, and he said, quite pleasantly, "You're late. The sixth lamp went out at sunset."
